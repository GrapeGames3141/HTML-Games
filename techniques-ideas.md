# Rendering technique ideas

**Status:** all twelve have a first working version in `experiments/demo/demo.html` (2026-11-17). None is used in an arcade game yet.
**Updated:** 2026-11-17

The games so far draw with two approaches: pictures generated by maths (raymarched distance fields, as in Sitter) and procedural Gaussian splats (see `SPLAT-GUIDE.md`). This file lists other techniques for light, shadow, water, transparency and whole-frame effects that would be new to this repo. Each entry says what it is, how it works, what WebGL2 needs for it, and where it would fit.

A walkable world that shows all twelve together is built in `experiments/demo/demo.html`; [experiments/demo/plan.md](experiments/demo/plan.md) records how each one was done and where it differs from the description below. Reusable findings are in the "Multi-pass techniques" section of `SPLAT-GUIDE.md`. T1 was built on a top-down floor plan, not in screen space as described here.

## Shared groundwork

Several techniques need the same plumbing, so build it once.

- **Off-screen scene buffers.** Render colour, depth and normals to textures before the final image. T1, T2, T9 and T11 all read them.
- **Float render targets.** `EXT_color_buffer_float` (and `OES_texture_float_linear` for smooth sampling) are needed for T1, T5, T6, T7, T8 and T12. Check for them at start-up and fall back to half-float or to a simpler effect.
- **Ping-pong simulation.** T5, T6 and T12 keep their state in textures and step it by drawing from one texture into another. WebGL2 has no compute shaders, so everything is done with fragment shaders.
- **One lighting function.** Sun, ambient, tonemap and fog should be one shared piece of shader text used by splats, meshes and water, so nothing looks pasted in.

## Light

### T1. Radiance cascades

**What:** bounced light and soft glow from anything that emits light, with no noise and no baking. A lantern lights the wall, the wall lights the floor, and a glowing mushroom tints the rock beside it.

**How:** light arriving at a point is stored in several layers ("cascades"). Near layers have many probes that each look in few directions over a short distance. Far layers have few probes that each look in many directions over a long distance. The layers are merged from far to near, so each pixel gets light from all distances for a fixed cost. Introduced by Alexander Sannikov.

**In WebGL2:** the practical version works in screen space: rays march through the depth buffer and pick up colour from the lit scene. It is a handful of full-screen passes into float textures. Its limit is that light sources off screen do not contribute, so keep a dim ambient term underneath. A world-space version on a coarse 3D grid is possible for a small enclosed space and is a bigger job.

**Cost:** the heaviest item here. Run it at half resolution, and only where it matters (interiors, night, caves).

### T2. Volumetric light shafts

**What:** visible beams of sunlight in mist, dust or murky water, broken by whatever stands in the way.

**How:** for each pixel, step along the view ray from the camera to the surface. At each step, ask the sun's shadow map whether that point is lit, and add a little fog light if it is. Jitter the starting offset per pixel and blur the result so 16 to 24 steps are enough.

**In WebGL2:** needs T3's shadow map and the depth buffer. Render at half resolution and upsample.

**Compared with now:** Spearfish fakes shafts in the backdrop. These would be real: walk behind a tree and the beam is cut.

## Shadow

### T3. Shadow maps from splats

**What:** true cast shadows for splat scenes, including the dappled light under a canopy.

**How:** draw the splats a second time from the sun's point of view into a depth texture. When shading a splat, compare its distance from the sun with what the depth texture recorded. Sampling several nearby texels softens the edge, and because splats already have soft, ragged edges the shadows come out looking painted.

**In WebGL2:** a depth texture and one extra pass over the static splats. Two or three nested maps (near, middle, far) keep shadows sharp close to the player. Static scenery only needs its map redrawn when the sun moves or the player crosses into a new area.

**Compared with now:** First Light fakes canopy shadow with a noise mask. Real shadow maps make each leaf cluster cast its own patch, and animals and the player are shadowed too.

### T4. Distance-field soft shadows

**What:** shadows that are sharp where an object touches the ground and blur as they stretch away, with no shadow map.

**How:** if the scene can report "how far is the nearest surface from this point" (a signed distance field), march from the shaded point toward the light and keep track of the closest near miss. A ray that only just clears an object is in the soft edge of its shadow. Popularised for raymarching by Inigo Quilez.

**In WebGL2:** nearly free in a raymarched scene such as Sitter. For a splat or mesh scene, describe the big occluders as simple shapes (spheres, capsules, boxes, the terrain height function) and march against those. The same field gives cheap ambient occlusion.

## Water

### T5. Height-field ripple simulation

**What:** water that reacts. Every pebble, fish, footstep and hull makes rings that spread, cross, and bounce off the banks.

**How:** the water surface is a grid of heights stored in a texture. Each step, every cell moves toward the average of its neighbours, with a little damping. That is the whole simulation. A disturbance is a small dent drawn into the texture. Normals for shading come from the height differences.

**In WebGL2:** two float textures swapped each frame, one small shader. A 256 or 512 grid per pool is plenty. A mask texture marks land so waves reflect off the shore.

**Why first:** smallest build on this list and the only one the player can poke at directly.

### T6. FFT ocean

**What:** open sea with proper swell: long rollers, short chop, crests that sharpen, and foam where waves pinch.

**How:** start from a statistical description of ocean waves (how much energy at each wavelength and direction for a given wind). Each frame, advance every wave's phase and use a fast Fourier transform to turn the whole set into a height and displacement map that tiles. Foam comes from where the surface folds over on itself. From Jerry Tessendorf's "Simulating Ocean Water".

**In WebGL2:** the transform runs as a series of fragment-shader passes on a 256 grid, which is 8 passes per direction. Use two or three tiles at different scales to hide the repeat. Buoyancy needs heights back on the CPU: either read back a small copy of the map or keep a few large Gerstner waves in JS that match the biggest swell.

**Compared with now:** Broadside sums a handful of waves written once in JS and GLSL. FFT gives thousands of waves, at the cost of that exact JS and GPU match.

### T7. True caustics

**What:** the bright moving net of light on the bottom of a pool, driven by the actual surface above it, so a ripple from a thrown pebble sends a ring of light across the bed.

**How:** treat the water surface as a grid of small triangles of light. Bend each corner's sunlight ray through the surface and find where it lands on the bottom. Where the landed triangle has shrunk, light is concentrated, so draw it brighter; where it has grown, dimmer. Add them all up into a light map.

**In WebGL2:** one pass that draws the surface grid into a float texture with additive blending, using T5 or T6's normals. The same idea run upward gives light dancing on a cave ceiling above a pool.

**Compared with now:** Spearfish uses a caustic pattern function that does not depend on the surface.

## Transparency

### T8. Weighted blended transparency

**What:** correct-looking overlapping glass, smoke, water spray and leaves without sorting anything back to front.

**How:** draw all transparent things in any order into two buffers: one adds up colour weighted by opacity and nearness, the other multiplies together how much background survives. A final pass combines them. It is an approximation, and a good one except where very different strong colours overlap. From McGuire and Bavoil.

**In WebGL2:** needs two float targets with different blend modes. WebGL2 only allows that with the `OES_draw_buffers_indexed` extension; without it, draw the transparent things twice, once per buffer.

**Compared with now:** the splat games avoid sorting with alpha-to-coverage, which suits near-opaque daubs. This handles real see-through layers.

### T9. Refraction with absorption and dispersion

**What:** thick glass, crystal and clear water that bend what is behind them, tint more deeply where they are thicker, and show colour fringes at the edges.

**How:** render the opaque scene to a texture first. When drawing a transparent object, look up that texture at a position shifted by the surface normal, which bends the background. Estimate thickness from the difference between the object's front and back depth, and darken by colour accordingly (Beer-Lambert absorption). Do the lookup three times with slightly different bend strengths for red, green and blue to get dispersion.

**In WebGL2:** needs the off-screen colour and depth buffers. A back-face depth pass gives thickness for closed meshes. For water, thickness is the depth of the bed below the surface.

### T10. Thin-film iridescence

**What:** the sliding rainbow on soap bubbles, oil on a puddle, beetle shells and some feathers.

**How:** light reflecting from the top and bottom of a very thin layer interferes with itself. Which colours are strengthened depends on the layer's thickness and the viewing angle. In a shader that is a small function from angle and thickness to a colour, with thickness varied by noise so the colours swirl.

**In WebGL2:** no special requirements. It is a few lines in a fragment shader, so it is the cheapest entry here.

## Whole frame

### T11. Painterly post filter (Kuwahara)

**What:** a filter over the finished image that turns it into flat dabs of colour with crisp edges between them, like oil paint.

**How:** for each pixel, look at several neighbouring regions around it, pick the one with the least variation, and use its average colour. The anisotropic version stretches those regions along the local direction of edges, which gives strokes that follow shapes.

**In WebGL2:** the basic version is one full-screen pass. The anisotropic version is three or four. Strength can vary with depth so the distance is looser than the foreground.

**Why it matters here:** it could make mesh objects and splat terrain read as one painting, which is the open question in the Slingshot note.

## Simulation

### T12. GPU particle fluid

**What:** thousands of particles that behave like liquid, mud or sand: pouring, pooling, splashing, piling up.

**How:** each particle is pushed away from neighbours when crowded and pulled along with them when not, so the set keeps a roughly constant density and flows. Position based fluids (Macklin and Müller) is the usual stable method. To draw it as liquid and not as dots, render the particles as spheres into a depth buffer, blur that depth, and shade the smooth surface that results.

**In WebGL2:** the hard part is finding neighbours without compute shaders. Options, easiest first: simulate a few thousand particles in JS and only draw on the GPU; keep the fluid on a grid (the T5 idea extended to shallow-water flow); or sort particles into a grid texture on the GPU, which is the most work.

**Honest note:** the riskiest item on the list. Plan a simpler stand-in (sprite spray plus T5 ripples) in case it does not hold frame rate.

## Others noted for later

Not part of the twelve, but worth remembering: screen-space reflections for wet ground, light probes for cheap bounce on moving objects, flow maps for directed stream currents, reaction-diffusion patterns for lichen and coral textures, and temporal accumulation to spread expensive effects over several frames.
